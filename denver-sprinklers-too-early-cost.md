In Denver, it’s best to wait until after Mother's Day, typically mid-May, to turn on your sprinkler system. Our region's last average freeze date hovers around May 15th. Activating your system earlier, especially in March or April, seriously risks expensive freeze damage to pipes, valves, and your backflow preventer.

## The Temptation of Early Spring: What Feels Right Isn't Always Safe

Every year it’s the same story. March rolls in with a few glorious, 70-degree days, the snow melts, and you start seeing a few dry, brown spots on the lawn. The urge to fire up the sprinkler system is powerful. You see a neighbor do it, and suddenly you feel like you're behind.

I get it. After a long winter, we’re all eager for green grass. But in my years of working on systems from Capitol Hill to Highlands Ranch, I can tell you that those early warm spells are Denver’s biggest head fake. The ground is still frozen just a few inches down, and our notoriously unpredictable Front Range weather means a hard, pipe-bursting freeze is often just a day or two away. Responding to that warm-weather itch is the single most common and costly mistake we see homeowners make.

## The Hidden Dangers of a March Startup: Burst Pipes and Damaged Backflows

Turning your system on too early isn't just a small risk; it's a gamble with expensive consequences. When you charge the lines with water and the temperature drops below 32°F overnight, that water expands with incredible force.

A professional winterization blowout is thorough, but it never removes 100% of the moisture. Pockets of water remain in low spots, valves, and elbows. When that residual water freezes, it's bad. But when you fill the entire system with water during a premature startup, a freeze is catastrophic.

Here’s what we see every April and May:
*   **Cracked Backflow Preventers:** This is the most common and costly repair. That brass assembly above ground (your PVB) is the first thing to freeze and crack. Replacing a backflow preventer can easily run you hundreds of dollars.
*   **Burst Pipes:** Underground PVC lines might seem protected, but a hard freeze can snap them, leading to underground geysers that waste thousands of gallons of water and require digging up your lawn.
*   **Shattered Valves:** The plastic bodies of your underground valves in the green boxes are no match for expanding ice. A single cracked valve means digging up the valve box and a multi-hundred dollar repair.
*   **Broken Sprinkler Heads:** Water trapped in the body of a high-end Rain Bird or Hunter rotor can crack the casing from the inside out.

Last spring, we were called out to a beautiful home in Parker that had a massive swamp in the front yard. The owner had turned the system on during a warm week in early April, only for a 25-degree night to freeze and split the main line three feet from the house. It was an expensive, muddy mess that could have been completely avoided.

## Denver's True Spring: Understanding Our Average Last Freeze Date

Meteorologists and old-time gardeners all agree: the safe point for planting and irrigation in the Denver metro area is after Mother's Day. The official average last freeze date for Denver is around **May 5-15**. This isn't just a suggestion; it's a date based on decades of climate data.

Why is this date so critical? At our 5,280-foot elevation, we lose heat rapidly once the sun goes down. A beautiful 65-degree afternoon can plummet into the high 20s overnight, especially with clear skies. Rushing to turn on your sprinklers in April is betting against historical weather patterns, and it’s a bad bet. A little hand-watering on a few trouble spots is a far better solution than paying for major repairs.

## What You CAN Do Now: Productive Spring Preparation (Without the Water)

Just because it's too early to turn the water on doesn't mean you can't be productive. Use these warmer days to do a "dry" system checkup.

*   **Walk Your Zones:** Visually inspect every sprinkler head. Did a snowplow shear one off near the curb? Did frost heave push any bodies up out of the ground?
*   **Clear the Area:** Use a trowel to cut back any overgrown turf or mulch that’s covering your sprinkler heads. A clear head pops up and retracts properly.
*   **Check Your Controller:** Plug in your controller and make sure the screen and buttons work. This is also a great time to research upgrading to a smart, weather-based controller that can save you water and money.
*   **Plan Your Season:** Look up your local watering rules. Denver Water customers, for example, can’t water between 10 a.m. and 6 p.m. from May 1 to October 1. Knowing the rules now helps you program your controller correctly later.

The best way to ensure your system is ready for the season without risking damage is to schedule a professional startup. We recommend booking your appointment for the last week of April or, even better, the first two weeks of May.

When a technician from **5280 Sprinklers** performs a startup, it's more than just turning a valve. Don't let a false spring fool you into a costly repair. Be patient, wait for May, and let your sprinkler system start the season safely and efficiently.

## FAQ

### Is it ever safe to turn my sprinkler system on in March in Denver?
No, it is not safe. While we may get warm days, the risk of a hard freeze is extremely high through March and April. The potential cost of repairs far outweighs the benefit of a few early waterings.

### What happens if my sprinkler pipes freeze after I've turned the system on?
When water in the pipes freezes, it expands and can crack PVC pipes, valves, sprinkler heads, and the expensive brass backflow preventer. This often results in major leaks, some of which may be underground and not immediately obvious.

### When is the absolute earliest I should consider a sprinkler startup in Denver?
The absolute earliest we advise even thinking about it is the last week of April, but this is still risky. The safest and most recommended time is after Mother's Day, or around May 15th, to be clear of the average last freeze date.

### I already turned my system on by mistake, what should I do now?
Immediately shut off the water supply to the sprinkler system at its main shut-off valve (usually in the basement). Then, call a professional like **5280 Sprinklers** to have the system properly drained or re-winterized to prevent damage if a freeze is in the forecast.

### How can I tell if my sprinkler system has freeze damage without turning it on?
You can visually inspect the above-ground backflow preventer for any visible cracks in the brass body or plastic handles. Unfortunately, most freeze damage to underground pipes and valves will only become apparent when you turn the water on and see geysers, wet spots, or a significant loss of pressure.
